### Area and volume
Consider a solid $E$ which lies in $\mathbb{R}^3$ between the planes $x = a$ and $x = b$. Suppose the cross sectional area of this solid at $x$ is given by $A(x)$. Then the volume of this solid is
$$
\int\limits_{a}^{b} A(x) dx
$$ 
Why? Well, this is the same thing as summing the surface areas ($A(x)$) over the entire $a,b$ range. This would give volume. Now let $E$ lie above a rectangular region $R = [a, b] \times [c,d]$ and below some surface given by $z = f(x,y)$. Since 
$$
A(x) = \int\limits_{c}^d f(x,y) \, dy
$$
This means that the volume of $E$ will be given b
$$
\text{vol } E = \int\limits_a^b\left(\int\limits_c^d f(x,y) \,\,dy\right)\,dx
$$

![[douleintegrals.png]]

Where this entire block is $E$. The cross section area $A(x)$ is essentially "what is the the surface area at a point $x$" where this surface area is given by the integral between $c$ and $d$ of $y$ under this curve $f(x,y)$ at the specific point $x$.

Note that we can do the same process but instead integrate the surfaces in the other direction. This would look like
$$
\text{vol } E = \int\limits_c^d\left(\int\limits_a^b f(x,y) \,\,dx\right)\,dy
$$
This can be interpreted as the integral of the surfaces given by $A(y)$ over the interval $c\rightarrow d$. Note this only applies whenever the base is rectangular.

## Example 11.1
Find the volume of the solid bounded above by the surface $z=xy$ that lies above the rectangle in the $xy$-plane given by $0 \leq x \leq 1$ and $1 \leq y \leq 2$

We see that this is a rectangular region. Setting up the integral
$$
\int\limits_1^2 \int\limits_0^1xy \ dx \ dy
$$
Now, evaluate the internal integral
$$
\int\limits_0^1xy \ dx = \frac{y}{2}x^2 \Big|_0^1 = \frac{1}{2}y
$$
Now the outer integral with with this expression 
$$
\int\limits_1^2 \frac{1}{2}y \ dy = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{y^2}{2}\right)\Big|_1^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{3}{4}
$$
Thus the area of this region is $\frac{3}{4}$

# 11.1.1 Double Integrals
## 11.1 Partitions
Let $f(x,y)$ be a function over the rectangle $R = [a,b] \times [c,d]$. A partition of $R$ is a collection of rectangles $R_ij = [x_{i-1}, x_i] \times [y_{j-1},y_j]$ for which $a = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{n-1} < x_n = b$ is a partition of $[a,b]$, and $c = y_0 < y_1 < \dots < y_{n-1} < y_n = d$ is a partition of $[c,d]$. Let ${\bf c}_{ij}$ be a point in the rectangle $R_{ij}$. Then the quantity
$$
\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^n f({\bf c}_{ij}) \Delta A_{ij}
$$
Where $\Delta A_{ij} = \Delta x_{i} \Delta y_j$ is the area of the rectangle $R_{ij}$ is called a Riemann sum of $f$ on $R$ corresponding to this partition of $R$. $f$ is integrable on $R$ if the limit of the Riemann sums as $(\Delta x_i \Delta y_j) \rightarrow (0,0)$ approach some real number. The limit of these Riemann sums is denoted by 
$$
\iint\limits_R f(x,y) \ dA
$$
## Theorem 11.1 
Suppose $D$ is a bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ that is contained in a closed rectangle $R = [a,b] \times [c,d]$. Let $f$ be a function defined on $D$. Assume $g$ is the extension of $f$ to $R$ defined by 
$$
g(x,y) = 
\begin{cases}
 f(x,y) & \text{if} (x,y) \in D \\
 0 & \text{else}
\end{cases}
$$
Then f is integrable over $D$ if and only if $g$ is integrable over $R$, in which case
$$
\iint\limits_R f(x,y) \ dA = \iint\limits_R g(x,y) \ dA
$$
### Rationale
Ok, so this is basically talking about how we can still integrate a 3D region when the base is not rectangular. We can essentially complete the region into a square and ignore the added parts by letting the function equal zero at those parts so that they don't contribute to the overall volume.

## Example 11.2
Let $R$ be a triangle in the $xy$-plane whose vertices are $(0,0),(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Evaluate the volume of the solid bounded above the plane $z = x + y$ that lies above the region $R$.

![[triangleregion.png]]

First setup the internal integral
$$
\int\limits_y^1 x+y \ dx
$$
Essentially, we are finding the area at some "fixed $y$", so it really is just the region between the point $x=y$ and $1$. Evaluating this integral
$$
\int\limits_y^1 x+y \ dx = \frac{1}{2}x^2 + xy \Big|^1_y = \frac{1}{2} + y  - \frac{3}{2}y^2
$$
Next, setup the outer integral with this expression and evaluate
$$
\int\limits_0^1 \frac{1}{2} + y  - \frac{3}{2}y^2 \ dy = \Big[\frac{1}{2}y + \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \frac{1}{2} y^3 \Big|^1_0 = \frac{1}{2} \Big[ y + y^2 - y^3 \Big|_0^1 = \frac{1}{2}
$$
Thus the volume is $\frac{1}{2}$. Alternatively, you could setup the double integral as
$$
\int\limits_0^1 \int\limits_0^x x+y \ dy \ dx
$$

## 11.2 Zero area
Let $X$ be a subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$. We say $X$ has zero area if for every $\epsilon > 0$ there is a sequence of closed rectangles $R_1, R_2, \dots$ for which 
$$
X \subseteq \bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty} R_n
$$
and the sum of the areas $R_n$'s is less than $\epsilon$.

Essentially, if we can cover a set of points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ which increasingly small rectangles whose areas add up to be less than any arbitrary positive $\epsilon$, then the thing has zero area since $\epsilon$ can be very small.
## Theorem 11.3
Let $f$ be a function that is bounded over a rectangle $R$ for which the points of discontinuity of $f$ in $R$ has zero area. Then $f$ is integrable over $R$
## 11.3 Elementary regions
A region $D$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is called elementary if it can be describes in one of the following ways
I.
$$
D = \{(x,y) \,|\, a \leq x \leq b, \delta_1(x) \leq y \leq \delta_2(x)\} 
$$
Where $\delta_i$ are continuous over $[a,b]$
II.
$$
D = \{(x,y) \,|\, \varphi_1(y) \leq x \leq \varphi_2(y), c \leq y \leq d\} 
$$
Where $\varphi_i$ are continuous over $[c,d]$
## Theorem 11.4 Fubini's Theorem
Let $f(x,y)$ be a function that is bounded on a bounded set $D$ of the $xy$-plane, and let $S$ be the set of all points of discontinuity of $f$ on $D$. Assume $S$ has zero area, and suppose every line parallel to the $x$-axis and every line parallel to the $y$-axis intersects $S$ in finitely many points. Then, $f$ is integrable over $D$ and 

1. If $D$ is of type I as described above, then 
$$
\iint\limits_D f \ dA = \int\limits_a^b \int\limits_{\delta_1(x)}^{\delta_2(x)} f(x,y) dy \ dx
$$ 
2. If D is of type II, then
$$
\iint\limits_D f \ dA = \int\limits_c^d \int\limits_{\varphi_1(x)}^{\varphi_2(x)} f(x,y) dx \ dy
$$
## Theorem 11.5 Properties of integrals
Suppose $f$ and $g$ are integrable functions over a subset $D$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $c$ is constant. Then,

1. $f + g$ is integrable and
$$
\iint\limits_D (f + g) \ dA = \iint\limits_D f \ dA \iint\limits_D g \ dA
$$
2. $cf$ is integrable and 
$$
\iint\limits_D cf \ dA = c \iint\limits_D f \ dA
$$
3. If $f \leq g$ over $D$, then 
$$
\iint\limits_D f \ dA \leq \iint\limits_D g \ dA
$$
4. $|f|$ is integrable over $D$ and 
$$
\left|\iint\limits_D f \ dA\right| = \iint\limits_D |f| \ dA
$$

## Example 11.3
Suppose $D$ is the region enclosed by $y = x^2$ and $y = \sqrt{x}$. Evaluate the following integral
$$
\iint\limits_D (xy + y^2) \ dA
$$
First, define the region $D$. This is basically the region under the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$ and $y = x^2$ or in other words, the region where
$$
x^2 \leq y \leq \sqrt{x}
$$
This is type I from 11.3. Now, define the boundary for $x$ i.e. when the two conditions for $y$ intersect. This gives
$$
x^2 = \sqrt{x} \,\,\,\, \Rightarrow \,\,\,\, x=0 \text{ or } x=1
$$
So $0 \leq x \leq 1$. This means that the entire region $D$ will be
$$
D = \,{(x,y) \,|\, x^2 \leq y \leq \sqrt{x} \text{ and } \, 0 \leq x \leq 1 \,}
$$
So, evaluating the integral over this region
$$
\int\limits_0^1 \int\limits_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} (xy + y^2) \ dy \ dx = \int\limits_0^1 \int\limits_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} xy \ dy \ dx + \int\limits_0^1 \int\limits_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} y^2 \ dy \ dx
$$
For the first integral,
$$
\int\limits_0^1 \int\limits_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} xy \ dy \ dx = \int\limits_0^1 x\frac{1}{2}y^2 \Big|_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} \ dx = \int\limits_0^1 \left[\frac{1}{2}x^2 - \frac{1}{2}x^5 \right] \ dx = \frac{1}{2} \Big[\frac{1}{3}x^3 - \frac{1}{6}x^6\Big|_0^1 = \frac{1}{12}
$$
For the second integral
$$
\int\limits_0^1 \int\limits_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} y^2 \ dy \ dx = \int\limits_0^1 \frac{1}{3} y^3 \Big|_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{3} \int\limits_0^1 x^{\frac{3}{2}} - x^6 \ dx = \frac{1}{3} \Big[\frac{2}{5}x^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{1}{7}x^7 \Big|_0^1 = \frac{3}{35}
$$
For the total area
$$
\frac{1}{12} + \frac{3}{35} = \frac{71}{420} \approx 0.169
$$
