## 13.2 Path-independent line integrals
A vector field ${\bf F}$ is said to have path-independent [line integrals](12.3%20Scalar%20line%20integrals) if
$$
\oint_{C_1} {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s} = \oint_{C_2} {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s}
$$
For $C_1 \neq C_2$ but they have the same endpoints

i.e. the value of the line integral is only dependent on the starting and endpoints of a curve. An example of a vector field that does this is gravity.
## Theorem 13.3
A vector field ${\bf F}$ has path-independent line integrals if and only if I
$$
\oint_C {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s} = 0
$$
for every simple (see [[7.4 Curves in Rn (derivatives)]]), piecewise continuously differentiable, [closed](13.1%20Green's%20theorem.md) curve $C$ in the domain of ${\bf F}$ 

i.e. the line integral is zero if $C$ has the same starting and end points, because like ofc.
## 13.3 Potential functions 
A continuous vector field ${\bf F}$  is said to be conservative if ${\bf F} = \nabla f$ for some continuously differentiable real-valued function $f$ . We call $f$ a potential function of ${\bf F}$ 

i.e. if ${\bf F}$ is the gradient of *some* function $f$, then it is a conservative vector field
## Theorem 13.4 Fundamental theorem of line integrals
Suppose ${\bf F}$ is a continuous vector field over an open connected subset $U$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. ${\bf F}$ is conservative on $U$ , if and only if ${\bf F}$ has path-independent line integrals over curves in $U$. Furthermore, if $C$ is a piecewise continuously differentiable curve in $U$ from point $A$ to point $B$ and ${\bf F} = \nabla f$ . Then 
$$
\oint_C {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s} = f(B) - f(A) 
$$

## 13.4 Simply connected regions
A region $U$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$ is called simply connected if it is connected and every simple closed curve in $U$ can be continuously shrunk to a point while remaining in $U$ . In other words, if ${\bf x} : [a, b] → U$ is a parametrization of a simple closed curve, then there is a continuous function $φ : [a, b] × [0, 1] → U$ for which $φ(t, 0) = {\bf x}(t)$ for all $t$, and $φ(t, 1)$ is constant.

Ok all this means is that $U$ has no holes
## 13.5 Cross products
Let ${\bf u} = (u_1, u_2, u_3)$, ${\bf v} = (v_1, v_2, v_3)$ be two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The cross product ${\bf u} × {\bf v}$ is defined as
$$
{\bf u} \times {\bf v} = \det
\begin{pmatrix}
{\bf i} & {\bf j} & {\bf k} \\
u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\
v_1 & v_2 & v_3
\end{pmatrix}
$$
Weird definition but in a nutshell this cross product gives us a vector $\in \mathbb{R}^3$ that is orthogonal to both ${\bf u}$ and ${\bf v}$ because when you work out the determinant you essentially get the solution to asking "what is some vector that if I take the dot product with ${\bf u}$ AND ${\bf v}$ both compute to 0?"

## Theorem 13.5 Properties of cross products
Here are some properties of cross products

1. ${\bf u} \times {\bf v}$ is orthogonal to both ${\bf u}$ and ${\bf v}$

2. ${\bf v} \times {\bf u} = -({\bf u} \times {\bf v})$ $\leftarrow$ **order matters**

3. ${\bf u} \times ({\bf v} + {\bf w}) = {\bf u} \times {\bf v} + {\bf u} \times {\bf w}$

4. $({\bf v} + {\bf w}) \times {\bf u} = {\bf v} \times {\bf u} + {\bf w} \times {\bf u}$

5. $|| {\bf u} \times {\bf v} || = ||{\bf u}|| \, ||{\bf v}|| \sin \theta$ where $\theta$ is the angle between the two vectors
## 13.5 Curl
Let ${\bf F}(x, y, z) = M (x, y, z){\bf i} + N (x, y, z){\bf j} + P (x, y, z){\bf k}$ be a vector field in $\mathbb{R}^3$ or ${\bf F}(x, y) = M (x, y) {\bf i} + N (x, y){\bf j}$ be a vector field in $\mathbb{R}^2$. In the three dimensional case, the curl of ${\bf F}$ denoted by $\text{curl} \ {\bf F}$  is defined as
$$
\text{curl} \ {\bf F} = \det
\begin{pmatrix}
	{\bf i} & {\bf j} & {\bf k} \\
	\frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\
	M & N & P
\end{pmatrix}
$$
With the 2D case, just let $P = 0$

Ok this definition is a little janky cause you can't really multiply $\frac{\partial}{\partial \text{ variable}}$ you just apply the partial operator to the specific function when computing. See Example 13.5

## Theorem 13.6
Suppose $U$ is a simply connected region in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$. Let ${\bf F}$ be a continuously differentiable vector field on $U$ . Then, ${\bf F} = \nabla f$ for some real-valued function $f$ if and only if $\text{curl} \  {\bf F} = 0$ on $U$

i.e. The curl of ${\bf F}$ is zero on $U$ $\iff$ ${\bf F}$ has some potential function $f$ $\iff$ ${\bf F}$ is a conservative vector field (at least on $U$)

## Example 13.5
Without finding a potential function, show the vector field
$$
{\bf F}(x,y,z) = (3x^2 + y\sin(xy)\,,\, 2y + x\sin(xy) \,,\, 2z+1) 
$$
Is conservative. Then, evaluate 
$$
\oint_C {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s}
$$
Where $C$ is a curve ${\bf x}(t) = (t^2, e^t, 2t)$ from $(0,1,0)$ to $(1, e, 2)$

Since we can't use a potential function, we will have to show the curl of this thing is zero. Computing the determinant definition, we get
$$
{\bf i}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y} P - \frac{\partial}{\partial z} N \right) 
-
{\bf j}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} P - \frac{\partial}{\partial z} M \right)
+
{\bf k}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} N - \frac{\partial}{\partial y} M \right)
$$
When you work everything out you get $(0,0,0)$ not too difficult. By Theorem 13.6 this implies that ${\bf F}$ is a conservative vector field. 

Since ${\bf F}$ is a conservative vector field, we know
$$
\oint_C {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s} = f \circ {\bf x}(1) - f \circ {\bf x}(0)
$$
So we need to find the potential function for ${\bf F}$ (you could try to just integrate but it's not really possible). Ok, if we integrate $M$ with respect to $x$ we obtain
$$
\int 3x^2 + y\sin(xy) \ dx = x^3 - \cos(xy) + g(y,z)
$$
Meaning that $f(x,y,z) = x^3 - \cos(xy) + g(y,z)$. This means that
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x\sin(xy) + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}
$$
We know that $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ must be $2y + x\sin(xy)$, thus
$$
x\sin(xy) + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2y + x\sin(xy)
$$
Thus 
$$
\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2y 
\,\,\,\,\,\, \Rightarrow \,\,\,\,\,\,
\int \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} \ dy = \int 2y \ dy = y^2 + h(z)
$$
Meaning that the whole function is now $f(x,y,z) = x^3 - \cos(xy) + y^2 + h(z)$. You do the same process starting with $\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}$ and get that $h(z) = z^2 + z$. Not too difficult to see why. Thus, a potential function is
$$
f(x,y,z) = x^3 - \cos(xy) + y^2 + z^2 + z + C
$$
We can evaluate this function at the end points and obtain
$$
\oint_C {\bf F} \cdot d{\bf s} = f \circ {\bf x}(1) - f \circ {\bf x}(0) = 7-\cos\left(e\right)+e^{2}
$$
So this is what the integral evaluates to
