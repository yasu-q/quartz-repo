Suppose some function $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ is a continuously differentiable function and $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. We would like to find a relation between $\iint\limits_D f \ dA$ and $\iint\limits_{T(D)} f \ dA$. Let $T(x,y) = (u,v)$. Thus, we must find a relation between $dx \ dy$ and $du \ dv$.
## 12.1 Jacobian of $T$
Let $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ be a continuously differentiable function given by $T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))$. The Jacobian (See 8.4 in [[8.2 Derivatives and differentials]]) of $T$ is given by 
$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \det
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\
	\frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} 
\end{pmatrix*}
$$
## Theorem 12.1 Change of variables
Let $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ be a $C^1$ function given by $T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))$. Suppose $D$ and $T(D)$ are elementary regions in the $uv$ and $xy$ planes, respectively. Assume $f$ is integrable over $T(D)$, then
$$
\iint\limits_{T(D)} f(x,y) \ dA = \iint\limits_{T(D)} f(u,v) \left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\ dA
$$
The fractional partial thing is the Jacobian's determinant, see [this](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix_and_determinant).

![https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hhFzJvaY__U&t=332s&ab_channel=SerpentineIntegral](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hhFzJvaY__U&t=332s&ab_channel=SerpentineIntegral)

The above theorem is also valid if $D$ and $T(D)$ can be broken into elementary regions. Additionally, this theorem is often summarized as simply
$$
dx \ dy = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \ du \ dv
$$
### Polar coordinate change of variables
(see video) Note that $dx \ dy = r \ dr \ d\theta$. This is the change of variables formula for polar coordinates. This is because
$$
x = r \cos\theta 
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
y = r \sin\theta
$$
$$
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\frac{\partial x}{\partial r} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} \\
	\frac{\partial y}{\partial r} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} 
\end{pmatrix*}
=
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\cos \theta & -r\sin\theta \\
	\sin \theta & r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix*}
$$
Take the determinant
$$
\det
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\cos \theta & -r\sin\theta \\
	\sin \theta & r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix*}
= r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r
$$
So the scaling factor is just $r$

### Cylindrical and spherical change of variables
For every point $P \in \mathbb{R}^3$ we assign a triple $(r,\theta,z)$ called the cylindrical coordinates of $P$ where $(r, \theta)$ are the polar coordinates of the point $(x,y)$

![[cylindrical.png]]

Thus the conversion is given buy 
$$
x = r\cos(\theta) \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
y = r\sin(\theta) \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
z = z
$$
Similarly, the spherical coordinates of $P$ are given by a triple $(\rho, \varphi, \theta)$ where $\rho$ is the distance of $P$ from the origin and $\varphi$ and $\theta$ and the angles made with different planes

![[Pasted image 20241113134336.png]]

The conversions are 
$$
x = \rho \sin(\theta) \cos(\varphi) \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
y = \rho \sin(\theta) \sin(\theta) \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
z = \rho \cos(\theta) 
$$
And $r = \rho \sin(\theta)$. The Jacobian coefficients (computation left as an exercise to the reader) are then
Cylindrical:
$$
dx \ dy \ dz = r \ dr \ d\theta \ dz
$$
Spherical:
$$
dx \ dy \ dz = \rho^2 \sin(\varphi) \ d\rho \ d\varphi \ d\theta
$$

## Example 12.4 
Evaluate the volume of a sphere of radius $a$.
### Using cylindrical cords
Setup the integral in the order
$$
dz \ dr \ d\theta
$$
The bottom boundary for $z$ will then be given by $- \sqrt{a^2-x^2-y^2} = - \sqrt{a^2-r^2}$ and the top boundary will then be $\sqrt{a^2-r^2}$. The lower bound for $r$ is then $0$ and the upper $a$ and finally $\theta$ ranges from $0$ to $2\pi$. The Jacobian coefficient of cartesian to cylindrical is given by $r$, this gives the following triple integral
$$
\int\limits_0^{2\pi} \int\limits_{0}^{a} \int\limits_{-\sqrt{a^2-r^2}}^{\sqrt{a^2-r^2}} r \ dz \ dr \ d\theta
$$
Evaluating the inner integral
$$
\int\limits_{-\sqrt{a^2-r^2}}^{\sqrt{a^2-r^2}} r \ dz = zr \Big|_{z = -\sqrt{a^2-r^2}}^{z=\sqrt{a^2-r^2}} = 2r\sqrt{a^2-r^2}
$$
Evaluating the central integral using $u = 1-r^2$
$$
\int\limits_{0}^{a} 2r\sqrt{u} \ \frac{du}{-2r} = - \int\limits_{0}^{a} \sqrt{u} \ du = -\frac{2}{3}(a^2 - r^2) \Big|_{r=0}^{r=a} = \frac{2}{3}a^3
$$
Now for the outer integral
$$
\frac{2}{3} \int\limits_{0}^{2\pi} a^3 \ d\theta = \theta a^3 \Big|_{\theta = 0}^{\theta=2\pi} = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3
$$
Which is exactly the formula for a sphere.
### Using spherical cords
Setup the integral in the order
$$
d\rho \ d\varphi \ d\theta
$$
The lower bound for $\rho$ is $0$ (remember, $\rho$ is the distance of the point from the origin) and the upper bound is $a$. We then integrate this over $\varphi$ from $0$ to $\pi$ and revolve this around $\theta$ from $0$ to $2\pi$. The Jacobian coefficient for cartesian to spherical is given by $\rho^2 \sin(\varphi)$, so we have the following triple integral
$$
\int\limits_0^{2\pi} \int\limits_{0}^{\pi} \int\limits_{0}^{a} \rho^2 \sin(\varphi) \ d\rho \ d\varphi \ d\theta
$$
For the first integral
$$
\int\limits_{0}^{a} \rho^2 \sin(\varphi) \ d\rho = \frac{1}{3}\rho^3\sin(\varphi) \Big|_{0}^{a} = \frac{1}{3}a^3 \sin(\varphi)
$$
For the central integral
$$
\frac{1}{3} \int\limits_{0}^{\pi} a^3 \sin(\varphi) \ d\varphi = \frac{1}{3} \Big[ -a^3\cos(\varphi) \Big|_{\varphi = 0}^{\varphi = \pi} = \frac{2}{3}a^3 
$$
Now the final integral
$$
\frac{2}{3} \int\limits_0^{2\pi} a^3 \ d\theta = \frac{2}{3} \Big[ \theta a^3 \Big|_{\theta = 0}^{\theta = 2\pi} = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3
$$
Which again, is the volume of a sphere
