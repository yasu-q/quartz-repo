Suppose some function $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ is a continuously differentiable function and $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. We would like to find a relation between $\iint\limits_D f \ dA$ and $\iint\limits_{T(D)} f \ dA$. Let $T(x,y) = (u,v)$. Thus, we must find a relation between $dx \ dy$ and $du \ dv$.
## 12.1 Jacobian of $T$
Let $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ be a continuously differentiable function given by $T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))$. The Jacobian (See 8.4 in [[8.2 Derivatives and differentials]]) of $T$ is given by 
$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \det
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\
	\frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} 
\end{pmatrix*}
$$
## Theorem 12.1 Change of variables
Let $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ be a $C^1$ function given by $T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))$. Suppose $D$ and $T(D)$ are elementary regions in the $uv$ and $xy$ planes, respectively. Assume $f$ is integrable over $T(D)$, then
$$
\iint\limits_{T(D)} f(x,y) \ dA = \iint\limits_{T(D)} f(u,v) \left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\ dA
$$
The fractional partial thing is the Jacobian's determinant, see [this](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix_and_determinant).

![https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hhFzJvaY__U&t=332s&ab_channel=SerpentineIntegral](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hhFzJvaY__U&t=332s&ab_channel=SerpentineIntegral)

The above theorem is also valid if $D$ and $T(D)$ can be broken into elementary regions. Additionally, this theorem is often summarized as simply
$$
dx \ dy = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \ du \ dv
$$
### Polar coordinate change of variables
(see video) Note that $dx \ dy = r \ dr \ d\theta$. This is the change of variables formula for polar coordinates. This is because
$$
x = r \cos\theta 
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
y = r \sin\theta
$$
$$
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\frac{\partial x}{\partial r} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} \\
	\frac{\partial y}{\partial r} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} 
\end{pmatrix*}
=
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\cos \theta & -r\sin\theta \\
	\sin \theta & r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix*}
$$
Take the determinant
$$
\det
\begin{pmatrix*}
	\cos \theta & -r\sin\theta \\
	\sin \theta & r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix*}
= r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r
$$
So the scaling factor is just $r$
