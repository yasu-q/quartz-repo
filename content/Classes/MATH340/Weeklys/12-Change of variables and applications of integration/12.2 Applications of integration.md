## 12.2 Average value of a function
Much like in single variable calculus, where the average value of a function over an interval $[a, b]$ is given by
$$
\frac{1}{b - a} \int\limits_{a}^{b}f(x)dx
$$
The average value of a function $f(x,y)$ over a region $D$ is given by
$$
\frac{1}{D_{\text{area}}} \iint\limits_D f(x,y) \ dA
$$
Where $D_{\text{area}}$ can be calculated by integrating $1$ over $D$. Similarly, the average value of a function $f(x,y,z)$ over a region $E \in \mathbb{R}^3$ can be found via
$$
\frac{1}{E_{\text{volume}}} \iiint\limits_{E} f(x,y,z) \ dV
$$
Where again, volume is found by integrating $1$ over $E$. This 'formula' extends into $\mathbb{R}^n$.
## 12.3 Moment
The moment of a point mass $m$ located at a point $x$ on the number line is given by
$$
mx
$$
If $n$ point masses at located at $x_n$ points, the total moment of these masses is then given by
$$
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}m_i x_i
$$
## 12.4 Center of mass
The center of a mass of a finite number of point masses on a number line is a location $x_j$ such that if a point mass with its mass equal to the sum of the finite point passes were located at $x_j$, then it would have the same amount of moment as the finite point masses
## 12.5 2D moment
A point mass $m$ located a point $(x,y)$ has a moment in respect to the $x$-axis of 
$$
my
$$
Similarly, its moment in respect to the $y$-axis is given by 
$$
mx
$$
Just break the moment up into its component parts not too difficult. This also means that we can find a center of mass of point passes in a $2D$ plane
## 12.6 2D Moments (again)
The component moments of $n$ point masses at location $(x_i, y_i)$ is given by 
$$
M_x = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} m_iy_i 
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
M_y = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} m_ix_i 
$$
## Theorem 12.2
The center of mass of point masses $m_1, . . . , m_n$ located at $(x_1, y_1), . . . , (x_n, y_n)$ is $(x, y)$, where
$$
\bar{x} = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} m_ix_i \left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} m_i\right)^{-1} 
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \text{ and } \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
\bar{y} = \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} m_iy_i \left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} m_i\right)^{-1} 
$$
## Theorem 12.3
Suppose an object is located at the region $D$ inside $\mathbb{R}^2$. Suppose $\delta(x, y)$ is the density of this object at point $(x, y)$. Then

1. The total mass of this object is $\iint\limits_{D} \delta(x,y) \ dA$ 

2. The total moments of this object with respect to the $x$ and $y$ axes are 
$$
\iint\limits_{D} y\delta(x,y) \ dA 
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \text{ and } \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
\iint\limits_{D} x\delta(x,y) \ dA 
$$

3. The center of mass of $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ is given by
$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{M}\iint\limits_{D} x\delta(x,y) \ dA 
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\, \text{ and } \,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
\bar{y} = \frac{1}{M}\iint\limits_{D} y\delta(x,y) \ dA 
$$
Where $M$ is the total mass of $D$
