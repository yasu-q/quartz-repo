## 13.1 Simple curves
A piecewise continuously differentiable path ${\bf x} : [a, b] → \mathbb{R}^n$ is said to be closed if ${\bf x}(a) = {\bf x}(b)$. 

It is said to be simple if ${\bf x}$ is one-to-one except possibly ${\bf x}(a)$ may be equal to ${\bf x}(b)$. A curve $C$ is the image of a function ${\bf x} : [a, b] → \mathbb{R}^n$ that is one-to-one except possibly at finitely many points of $[a, b]$. 

We say ${\bf x}$ is a parametrization of $C$. We say $C$ is closed or simple if it has a parametrization that has the corresponding property

i.e. a path is closed if the starting and end points are equal. A path is simple if it intersects at finitely many points
## Theorem 13.1 Green's Theorem
 Let $D$ be a closed bounded region in $\mathbb{R}^2$, whose boundary $\partial D$ consists of finitely many simple, closed, piecewise continuously differentiable curves. Suppose $\partial D$ is oriented in such a way that $D$ lies on the left as one traverses $\partial D$(it's counter-clockwise). Let $F = M {\bf i} + N {\bf j}$ be a continuously differentiable vector field on $D$. Then
$$
\int\limits_{\partial D} M \ dx + N \ dy = \iint\limits_{D} \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) \ dA
$$
i.e. we can apply this equality whenever 
1. $\partial D$ is closed
2. $F$ is continuously differentiable on **the entirety** of $D$, not just $\partial D$ 

### Note about notation
We often write
$$
\oint_C
$$
To indicate a [line integral](12.3%20Scalar%20line%20integrals) over a region $C$.

## Example 13.1 
Evaluate the following line integral
$$
\oint_C (x^2 - y^2) \ dx + (x^2 + y^2) \ dy
$$
Where $C$ is the boundary of the unit square (bottom left at (0,0) and top right at (1,1))  oriented **clockwise**.

There are two approaches to solving this problem. You can either split the square into 4 different parametrizations and compute 4 different line integrals, or you could apply Green's theorem! wow

First, note that $F$, the vector field, is continuous on $D$ and that $D$ is closed. Then, note that we need to negate the double integral since $D$ is traversed clockwise, noting that in the statement for Green's theorem we said $D$ is oriented counter-clockwise (we can do this because see [[12.4 Vector line integrals#12.11 Reparameterization orientation]])

This means we need
$$
-\iint\limits_{D} \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) \ dA
$$
First,
$$
\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = 2x
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = -2y
$$
And then we integrate this over $dA$ which is just a square, this yields a double integral
$$
-\int\limits_{0}^{1} \int\limits_{0}^{1} 2x + 2y \ dy \ dx
$$
Evaluating the inner integral
$$
\int\limits_{0}^{1} 2x + 2y \ dy = 2xy + y^2 \Big|_{y=0}^{y=1} = 2x + 1
$$
$$
-\int\limits_{0}^{1} 2x + 1 \ dx = - \big[ x^2 + x \big|_{x=0}^{x=1} = -2
$$
So this line integral evaluates to $-2$ by Green's theorem $\square$

## Example 13.2
Evaluate the area of a region enclosed by
$$
C = \left\{ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\right\}
$$
where $a,b > 0$. If we were to use a double integral to evaluate this, we would do
$$
\iint_{C_{\text{region}}} 1 \ dA
$$
However, we won't because this is a Green's theorem example. Thus, we want some $N$ and $M$ such that
$$
\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 1
$$
We will use $N = \frac{1}{2}x$ and $M = -\frac{1}{2}y$ because the symmetry is convenient. Thus using Green's theorem we can write the line integral
$$
\oint_C -\frac{1}{2}y \ dx + \frac{1}{2} x \ dy
$$
To parametrize $C$ which is an ellipse, we can use $x = a\cos t$ and $y = b \sin t$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$. This gives us the following:
$$
dx = -a \sin t \ dt
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
dy = b \cos t \ dt
$$
Plugging everything into the integral and solving
$$
\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi} -\frac{1}{2}(b\sin t)(-a \sin t) + \frac{1}{2}(a\cos t)(b \cos t) \ dt
\,\,\,\,\, \Rightarrow \,\,\,\,\,
\frac{1}{2} \int\limits_{0}^{2\pi} ab \sin^2 t + ab \cos^2 t \ dt
$$
This simplifies to 
$$
\Rightarrow \frac{1}{2}ab \int\limits_{0}^{2\pi} 1 \ dt = \pi ab
$$
Which is the exact formula for the area of an ellipse $\square$

## Theorem 13.2
Essentially states that the order of some surface $D \in \mathbb{R}^2$ can be found using $M(x,y)$ and $N(x,y)$ if they are continuously differentiable over $D$ and 
$$
\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 1
$$
And thus 
$$
D_{\text{Area}} = \oint_C M \ dx + N \ dy
$$
Where $C$ is the boundary of $D$ oriented counterclockwise. This also leads to a more general formula for the area of $D$ which is 
$$
D_{\text{Area}} = \frac{1}{2} \oint_C x \ dy - y \ dx
$$
