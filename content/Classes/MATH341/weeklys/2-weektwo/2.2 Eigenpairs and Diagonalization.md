Find $A^{100}{\bf v}$ where

First, multiplying the two,

A^{100}{\bf v} = 5^{100}{\bf v}
$$
Because $(5, {\bf v})$ is an eigen-pair of $A$

## 2.3 Eigen values and eigenvectors
Suppose $T : V \rightarrow V$ is a linear transformation and ${\bf v} \in V$ is a nonzero vector for which $T ({\bf v}) = \lambda{\bf v}$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{F}.$ We say $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue, ${\bf v}$ is an eigenvector, and the ordered pair $(\lambda, {\bf v})$ is called an eigenpair of $T$ . Similarly we define eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and eigenpairs of a square matrix $A$.
## Theorem 2.5
$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of a square matrix $A$ iff $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. See [[Note about eigenvectors]]
## 2.4 Characteristic polynomials
Given a square matrix $A$, the polynomial
$$
p(z) = \det(A - zI)
$$
is called the characteristic polynomial of $A$

Note that if $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, then $\det(zI − A) = (−1)^n \det(A − zI)$. Therefore, the roots of $\det(zI − A) = 0$ and $\det(A − zI) = 0$ are the same. In some textbooks, $\det(zI − A)$ is defined to be the characteristic polynomial of $A$
## 2.5 Diagonalizable matrices
A square matrix $A$ is said to be diagonalizable if there is a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ for which $A = P DP^{-1}$. In other words, a matrix is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix
## Theorem 2.6
A matrix $A \in M_n(\mathbb{F})$ is diagonalizable if and only if there is a basis for $\mathbb{F^n}$ consisting of eigenvectors of A. Furthermore, if $(\lambda_1, {\bf v}_1), . . . , (\lambda_n, {\bf v}_n)$ are eigenpairs of $A$ whose eigenvectors form a basis for $\mathbb{F}^n$, then $A = P DP^{−1}$, where
$$
D = 
\begin{pmatrix}
	\lambda_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
	0 & \lambda_2 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
	0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_{n-1} & 0 \\
	0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_n
\end{pmatrix}
$$
And also
$$
                                            
$$
### Semi-proof
$$
A = PDP^{-1} \,\,\Rightarrow\,\, AP = PD \,\,\Rightarrow\,\, A({\bf v}_1 \cdots {\bf v}_n) = ({\bf v}_1 \cdots {\bf v}_n)  \text{ diag}(\lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n)
$$
Looking at the left and right sides we see
$$
A{\bf v}_j \text{ is the jth row of } AP 
$$
$$
({\bf v}_1 \cdots {\bf v}_n)  \text{ diag}(\lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n) = \lambda_j{\bf v}_j \text{ is the jth row of } PD
$$
i.e. $A{\bf v}_j = \lambda_j{\bf v}_j$ so $(\lambda_j, {\bf v}_j)$ is an eigenpair of $A$. This process works backward IF $P$ is invertible. If $P$ is indeed invertible that means that its column vectors are L.I. Since there are $n$ column vectors, then they will form a basis for $\mathbb{F}^n$ 
### Note about notation
The diagonal entries of the matrix $D$ are denoted as $\text{diag}(\lambda_1, \cdots, \lambda)$ 

The process of finding an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ for which $A = P DP^{−1}$ is called diagonalizing matrix $A$

## Example 2.6
Diagonalize
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
and find $A^n$. First, lets find $A$'s eigenvalues using its [[#2.4 Characteristic polynomials|characteristic polynomial]]
$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = \det
\begin{pmatrix}
	2 - \lambda & -3 \\
	-4 & 1 - \lambda
\end{pmatrix}
=
(2 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda) - 12 = \lambda^2 - 3 \lambda - 10 
$$
$$
\Rightarrow (\lambda - 5)(\lambda + 2)
$$
So the eigen values of $A$ are $\lambda = 5, - 2$. Now, lets find the eigenvectors
$$
\lambda = 5, \, (A - \lambda I){\bf v} = {\bf 0} \,\,\Rightarrow\,\, 
\begin{pmatrix}
	-3 & -3 \\
	-4 & -4
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 
	v_1 \\
	v_2
\end{pmatrix}
= 0 \,\,\Rightarrow\,\,
{\bf v} = (-1, 1)
$$
So an eigenpair is $(5, (-1, 1))$. Now for $\lambda = -2$ 
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
	4 & -3 \\
	-4 & 3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 
	v_1 \\
	v_2
\end{pmatrix}
= {\bf 0}
\,\,\Rightarrow\,\, (-2, (3, 4))
$$
Is another pair. Note that both the eigen vectors found form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$. This means
$$
A = PDP^{-1} =
\begin{pmatrix}
	-1 & 3 \\
	1 & 4
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
	5 & 0 \\
	0 & -2
\end{pmatrix}P^{-1}
$$
Now, for $A^n$,
$$
A^2 = PDP^{-1}PDP^{-1} = PD^2IP^{-1} = PD^2P^{-1}
$$
$$
A^3 = A^2A=PD^2P^{-1}PDP^{-1} = PD^{3}P^{-1}
$$i.e.
$$
A^n = PD^nP^{-1} = P
\begin{pmatrix}
	5^n & 0 \\
	0 & -2^n
\end{pmatrix}
P^{-1}
$$
(we can raise entries to $n$ because $D$ is a diagonal matrix)

## Theorem 2.7
Every two similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial and therefore they have the same eigenvalues
### Proof
Suppose $A$ and $B$ are similar matrices where
$$
A = PBP^{-1}
$$
For some invertible matrix $P$. The [[#2.4 Characteristic polynomials|characteristic polynomial]] of $A$ is
$$
p(x) = \det(A - xI) = \det(PBP^{-1}-xI)
$$
$$
\Rightarrow \det(P(B - P^{-1}xIP)P^{-1}) = \det(P(B - xP^{-1}P)P^{-1})
$$$$
\Rightarrow \det(P(B - xI)P^{-1}) = \det(P)\det(B - xI)\det(P^{-1})
$$
$$
\Rightarrow \det(PP^{-1})\det(B - xI) = \det(B - xI)
$$
## Theorem 2.8 Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
Every polynomial $p(x)$ of degree $n$ with complex coefficients can be completely factored into linear terms. In other words, there are complex numbers $c, a_1, \cdots, a_n$ for which
$$
p(x) = c(x - a_1) \cdots(x - a_n)
$$
## Theorem 2.9
Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. Consequently, if an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, then it is diagonalizable 
## Theorem 2.10
Suppose $A$ is a diagonalizable matrix with $A = P DP^{−1}$, where $D = (\lambda_1, . . . , \lambda_n)$. Then 
$$
e^A = P \, \text{diag}(e^{\lambda_1} , \cdots, e^{\lambda_n} ) P^{−1}
