$$
\ker(A - \lambda I) \not\subseteq \ker(A - \lambda I)^2 \not\subseteq \cdots \not\subseteq \ker(A - \lambda I)^{k + 1} = \cdots
$$
## 3.3 Eigenvectors and eigenspaces
 For an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, every nonzero vector in $\ker (A − \lambda I)^n$ is called a generalized eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to eigenvalue $\lambda$. The vector space $\ker (A − \lambda I)^n$ is called the generalized eigenspace of $A$ corresponding to eigenvalue $\lambda$.

## 3.4 Multiplicity root
The multiplicity of a root $r$ of a polynomial $p(z)$ is $m$ if
$$
p(z) = (z - r)^mq(z)
$$
for some polynomial $q(z)$ with $q(r) \neq 0$
## Theorem 3.6
The dimension of the generalized eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda$ for a square matrix $A$ is the same as the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the characteristic polynomial of $A$

The above theorem implies that given an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ we can find a basis for $\mathbb{F}^n$ consisting of generalized eigenvectors of $A$ by finding a basis for each generalized eigenspace and putting all of these bases together
## 3.5 Jordan canonical form
A matrix is said to be in Jordan canonical form (or Jordan form for short) if it is a block matrix of the form
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
	B_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
	0 & B_2 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
	0 & 0 & \cdots & B_{n-1} & 0 \\
	0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & B_n
\end{pmatrix}
$$
where each $B_i$, called a Jordan block, is a matrix with an eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ on its main diagonal, 1’s immediately above the main diagonal, and zeros everywhere else. tsumari
$$
B_i = 
\begin{pmatrix}
	\lambda_i & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
	0 & \lambda_i & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
	\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\
	0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_i & 1 \\
	0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_i
\end{pmatrix}
$$

## Theorem 3.8
Let A be a matrix in $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ and $J$ be a matrix in Jordan form that is similar to $A$. Then, for every positive integer $k$, the number of Jordan blocks of $J$ with size at least $k \times k$ corresponding to an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is given by
$$
\dim \ker(A - \lambda I)^k - \dim \ker(A - \lambda I)^{k-1}
$$
Note $(A - \lambda I)^0 = I$ and has a kernel of dimension zero

Using the above theorem, we can find the Jordan form of any matrix rather easily, however finding the matrix $P$ in $A = PJP^{−1}$ is more difficult.
