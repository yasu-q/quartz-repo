Not all matrices can be diagonalized. Fortunately, we can do something close to diagonalization
## 3.1 Upper triangular matrix
A square matrix $A$ is said to be upper triangular if all of its entries below the main diagonal are zero
## Theorem 3.1 Block multiplication
Suppose matrices $A$ and $B$ are given as block matrices
$$
A = 
\begin{pmatrix}
	A_{11} & A_{12} \\
	A_{21} & A_{22}
\end{pmatrix}
\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,
B = 
\begin{pmatrix}
	B_{11} & B_{12} \\
	B_{21} & B_{22}
\end{pmatrix}
$$
where $A_{jk}, B_{jk}$ are themselves matrices. Assuming all appropriate multiplications and additions are defined we have
$$
AB = 
\begin{pmatrix}
	A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{12} & A_{11}B_{12} + A_{12}B_{22} \\
	A_{21}B_{11} + A_{22}B_{22} & A_{21}B_{12} + A_{22}B_{22}
\end{pmatrix}
$$
A similar result holds for matrices divided into blocks of matrices
## Theorem 3.2
 Every square matrix with complex entries is [[2.2 Eigenpairs and Diagonalization#2.5 Diagonalizable matrices|similar]] to an upper triangular matrix with complex entries
## 3.2 Polynomials with matrix inputs
Given a polynomial $p(x) = a_mx^m + \cdots + a_1x + a_0$ and an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ we define
$$
p(A) = a_mA^m + \cdots + a_1A + a_0I
$$
## Theorem 3.3 Cayley-Hamilton
Let $A$ be a square matrix, and $p(x)$ be the characteristic polynomial of $A$. Then, $p(A) = 0$
